Forecasting a sales pipeline: stage win chances and close dates

On 8 quarters of public tenders, CRM default win chances beat re-learned ones only by making up for wins dated outside the quarter, so fix close dates first.

Every quarter, a sales leader is asked how many open deals will close. The answer usually comes from the CRM, the sales team's database of deals. Each deal has a stage, with a win chance, and a close date, when the seller expects a decision. The forecast adds up the deals with a close date this quarter, at those chances. One common fix is to re-learn the chances from how past deals closed. I tested both ways on 8 quarters of Canadian government tenders, each treated as a deal in a supplier's pipeline.

What to do

Fix close dates, then re-learn the win chances. Until both are done, keep the CRM's default chances on the pipeline as it stands. They did better here, but by luck. Their optimism made up for wins the forecast never counted. Re-dating deals under those same chances would push the forecast too high.

Next quarter, for the revenue team

  • Close out stale deals, and give the live ones a real close date. Of the open deals dated outside the latest quarter, the 7,297 that had waited over a year for an award gave 2 wins. The other 3,829 gave 207, about half of that quarter's wins from open deals.
  • Check how often a buyer's tenders end in an award to anyone before you bid there. A tender with no award wastes every bid on it. Since August 2022, 62% of tenders at the National Research Council ended in a published award, and 19% at Shared Services Canada. Then weigh your own win rate and bid cost there before moving bids.
  • Plan the quarter against a range, not the forecast. In each of the 8 quarters, the defaults' forecast ran 3% to 50% above the wins from open deals. Test any range on your own past quarters before you plan on it.

What it is worth

Over the 8 quarters, the defaults missed by 84 wins a quarter on average, and the re-learned chances by 242. A typical win is a contract of about CA$195,000.

How I would put this to work with a team

  1. Week one, revenue operations. Export every deal with its stage and close-date history and the date each win was booked.
  2. Weeks two and three, an analyst. Replay the last 8 quarters as here, and count the wins from deals dated outside each quarter.
  3. Week four, the sales leader and finance. Publish both forecasts until the switch rule below is met.

The two numbers that matter

  1. The miss on all wins from open deals. Over the 8 quarters, the defaults missed those wins by 23% on average, and the re-learned chances by 61% on average. The defaults were too high every quarter, and the re-learned chances too low.
  2. The miss on the deals each forecast counts. Over the 8 quarters, the defaults missed the wins from deals dated in the quarter by 140% on average, too high. Over the same 8 quarters, the re-learned chances missed them by 24% on average, too low.

The two numbers measure misses on different wins, so they do not conflict.

What would change the conclusion

  • If under one in five wins from open deals came from deals dated outside the quarter, test re-learned chances next. Here, leaving out deals dated only by a past bid deadline, it was 21% to 32% of the wins from open deals in each of the 8 quarters. The lowest was just above the line.
  • If cleaner close dates do not bring your forecast closer within two quarters, dates alone are not the fix. Test dates and chances one at a time.
  • Switch from the defaults only to a forecast that beats them in at least 7 of your last 8 quarters. The best alternative here beat them in 4.

Limits

Tenders stand in for CRM deals, and tender dates for close dates. About half of the wins dated outside the quarter came from tenders with no date of their own, where a past bid deadline stood in.

A win is a published award, and tenders have no lost stage. Only 10,059 of the 27,589 tenders link to an award. The rest stay open unless cancelled, so deals waiting over a year for an award grew from 441 to 7,297 over the 8 quarters. An award made through a route not published here never counts, which may be why Shared Services Canada's rate is low.

I chose the stage mapping. I gave the CRM's 60% win chance to tenders open for bids, and its 80% to those awaiting an award. One stage either way, the defaults' average miss over the 8 quarters would be 16% or 52%. One stage lower, they ran low in 6 of the 8 quarters.

Some choices came after the results. Only the first of the two numbers was planned before them. The other, and every count of wins and deals dated outside the quarter, came after. The one-in-five line and the 7-of-8 rule are my judgment too.

Notices keep only their last version. Deals amended later held 29% to 44% of the wins from open deals in each of the 8 quarters. Leaving them out kept the forecasts' order, but the defaults ran too high in only 2 of the 8.

Awards are published late. That adds about 9 points to the re-learned chances' average miss over the 8 quarters. A CRM usually books wins sooner, so its gap would be smaller.

A third forecast came close. It used how long each deal had sat at its stage, with late awards counted. It missed by 16% on average over the 8 quarters, level with the defaults one stage lower, but I chose that correction after the results.

Few wins carry an amount. The typical contract is the median of the 41% of wins that carry one. Half of those amounts fall between CA$74,000 and CA$724,000.
